# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---
## 1.

Of 10 human-written pieces, including 5 plain ones (short sentences of around 10 words or fewer, using mostly common, everyday words), at most 1 gets the "likely AI" label, in every one of the 3 trials.

**Why this target:** I allow at most 1 because 0 is unrealistic (no detector can promise it), but more than 1 means too many real writers get accused. The writers most likely to be wrongly flagged are second-language writers like Amina, and a false accusation on their own work could make them leave the site. That costs far more than missing some AI text.


---

## 2.

I send 10 appeals, each using a real content_id from an earlier submission. All 10 must pass: the response returns the content_id and status "under_review", GET /log shows a new appeal entry with the writer's reasoning, the original decision entry is still in the log unchanged, and the status for that content_id is now "under_review". I also send 3 appeals with made-up content_ids, and all 3 must return 404 and write nothing to the log.

**Why this target:**

Unlike detection, recording an appeal is plain code, not a guess, so there's no reason for even one to fail. An appeal is the only way a wrongly flagged writer like Amina can push back. If her appeal silently disappears, she believes it's under review, but nobody ever looks at it. A fake content_id must return 404 so that fake appeals don't pollute the log.

---

## 3.

I score 5 clearly-AI pieces (text generated by a chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT and pasted in unedited) and 5 clearly-human pieces (casual writing by me or friends, such as texts or journal entries, messy and informal). In every one of the 3 trials, the average combined score of the AI pieces is at least 0.3 higher than the average combined score of the human pieces.

**Why this target:** If clearly-AI and clearly-human text score close together (say 0.55 and 0.45), both land in the unsure label, and the system can't tell anything apart. A gap of 0.3 matches the distance between my two thresholds (0.35 and 0.65), so clear cases can reach the human and likely AI labels. These are the easiest cases, so if my signals can't separate them, they won't separate the hard ones either.

---

## 4.

In every one of the 3 trials, at least 2 submitted pieces land in each of the three labels (human, unsure, likely AI). Separately, I feed these exact scores into scoring.py::score_to_label: 0, 0.34, 0.35, 0.36, 0.64, 0.65, 0.66 and 1. Every one returns exactly one of the three labels, with no error and no gap.

**Why this target:** A label that can never be reached is useless: if no real text ever gets "unsure", then a writer like Amina gets pushed into human or likely AI instead, even when the system isn't sure. I test the exact thresholds because that's where a gap can hide, and a score with no label would crash or show the writer nothing.

---

## 5.

I send 6 bad requests to POST /submit: text missing, text empty (""), text only spaces ("   "), text under 20 words, text over 5,000 words, and creator_id missing. All 6 must return 400 with an error message, none of them may be written to the log as a decision, and /ping must still answer afterward.

**Why this target:** Bad input should be stopped before it reaches the signals. Under 20 words, the score is too noisy to judge someone's writing fairly, so a short poem could get flagged by chance. Over 5,000 words, the request is slow and GPT-2 only reads the first 400 tokens anyway. If bad input got through, it could crash the service, or a junk score could be logged as a real decision about a real writer.
